Vocal Warm-Ups Before You Play a Voice Game

Hum Runner only needs a comfortable, steady hum; it is not a singing test. A short warm-up can help you find a relaxed sound and remember the low and high notes you will use for calibration. Keep it gentle and brief. You do not need to hit a target note, sing loudly, or extend your range.

Start with an easy breath

Before making sound, sit or stand comfortably and let your shoulders rest. Take a few ordinary, unforced breaths. There is no need to take the deepest breath you can or to hold it. Let the next exhale be easy and, if it feels comfortable, add a quiet hum near the middle of your speaking range.

Keep the sound soft enough that you could continue speaking normally afterward. A relaxed “mmm” is enough. Pause between attempts and notice whether your throat, jaw, and shoulders feel comfortable. If you feel tightness, stop and rest rather than trying to push through it.

Try a few steady hums

Hum one comfortable note for a couple of seconds, then rest. Repeat it a few times without increasing volume. The goal is simply to find a clear voiced tone that feels repeatable. You can try a gentle “ooo” if that shape is easier for you, but there is no single best vowel for every person or microphone.

Pitch detection works better with a tone that repeats than with a whisper or a breathy exhale. That does not mean you should force a strong sound. Use your normal, easy voice, and stop if the sound becomes uncomfortable.

Explore small pitch slides

When your hum feels easy, glide a little higher and then a little lower, staying within notes that are comfortable today. Move smoothly instead of leaping to the very top or bottom of your range. These small slides can help you notice how your voice changes pitch, which is useful when calibrating the game’s controls.

For calibration, the game asks for a low note and then a high note for a few seconds each. “Low” and “high” are relative to you. Pick notes you can sustain without strain, and leave some comfortable room between them. A wider span can make it easier for the game to map your voice across the screen, but there is no benefit to forcing an extreme note.

Keep the room and setup comfortable

Warm up in a quiet place if you can. Background sound may interfere with the pitch estimate, so reducing music or nearby conversation helps the game hear a clearer signal. Place your phone roughly 15–20 cm (6–8 in) from your mouth, with the microphone facing you. You do not need to shout or move the phone close to your lips.

Then follow the on-screen prompts and hold each note steadily. If calibration seems off, try again with a comfortable low note and a slightly higher but still easy note. The How to Play guide has more setup suggestions, and the FAQ covers microphone problems.

Take breaks and listen to your voice

A warm-up should leave you feeling comfortable, not tired. Keep the session short, take breaks, and do not compete with the game by singing louder or reaching for a note that hurts. If your throat feels sore, tight, or unusually tired, stop playing and let your voice rest. These are general comfort tips, not medical advice.

There is no need to prepare perfectly. A few gentle hums are plenty; if you would rather not use your voice, choose touch mode and play by dragging on the screen.
